I · The physics

A tone that exists only inside the head

Play a steady 200 Hz tone into the left ear and a 206 Hz tone into the right, through headphones, and you will hear something neither ear received: a soft pulsing at six beats per second. That pulse is a binaural beat. It is not in the air. It is assembled in the brainstem, where neurons of the superior olivary complex compare the timing of the signals from the two ears and register the difference between them.

The effect was first described by the Prussian physicist Heinrich Wilhelm Dove in 1839. In 1973 the biophysicist Gerald Oster drew it back into science with his Scientific American article “Auditory beats in the brain,” and proposed it as a tool for studying the auditory system. Binaural beats are heard best with carrier tones below about a thousand hertz and a difference between the ears of up to roughly thirty hertz, which places the beat squarely in the range of the brain’s own rhythms.

The central idea of entrainment is that the brain tends to follow a steady external rhythm. Present a beat at six hertz and the auditory system produces a measurable response at six hertz; the hope, and the hypothesis tested in laboratories ever since, is that the wider brain is drawn toward that rhythm too.

Left ear + right ear = the beat

Headphones are essential: each ear must receive only its own tone. Speakers mix the two in the air and the binaural effect disappears.

II · Why theta

The receptive state

Each band of the brain’s rhythm is associated with a different quality of consciousness. Theta is the one the contemplative traditions kept finding.

Beta · 12–30 Hz

The working mind

Alert, engaged, task-focused. The state the rites begin by leaving behind.

Alpha · 8–12 Hz

The doors close

Relaxed wakefulness, eyes closed, attention turned inward: the purification and settling.

Theta · 4–8 Hz

The threshold

The hypnagogic edge of sleep: vivid imagery, deep absorption, and in meditators, sustained concentration at the front of the head.

Gamma · 30+ Hz

The flash

High-frequency integration, reported at striking amplitude in some long-term meditators.

Theta is where the body can rest while the mind stays awake. It is the state the Hekhalot adept approached with head between the knees and whispered hymns; the state of the seeress on her high seat while the warding songs were sung; the state the shamanic drum was built to induce. Practitioners of contact work, from the American sound laboratories of the 1970s to the open-sky contact circles of the 1990s, describe the same thing: a theta-dominant mind is a receptive mind. Imagery arrives rather than being made. Impressions surface that the busy beta mind would talk over.

That is why entrainment runs through this library. Most of our rites carry theta-range binaural layers beneath their invocations and silences, and several add gamma-range layers for the moments of invocation and vision.

III · The sound laboratory

How sound became a door

In the late 1950s an American radio executive began, by his own account, to leave his body at night. He published the experience in 1971 and spent the rest of his life looking for a reliable, repeatable way back to it. His answer was sound. In a laboratory in rural Virginia he built layered binaural audio on the theory that the beat draws both hemispheres of the brain into one shared, coherent rhythm.

From this came a graded training method in which the listener climbs a numbered ladder of states, each anchored by its own binaural signature and its own verbal cues, so that with practice a state can be re-entered at will. Anyone who has studied the field knows the rungs by number.

In 1983 the method attracted the attention of United States Army intelligence. Lieutenant Colonel Wayne McDonnell was tasked with assessing it, and his report, dated 9 June 1983, tried to explain the technique in the language of physics and biomedicine: hemispheric synchronisation, brainwave coherence, and a holographic model of consciousness. The report was later released through the CIA’s declassified reading room and became one of the most widely read documents in its archive. One page, page 25, has never been found.

The 1983 assessment matters to this library for one reason: it showed that a structured sound environment, used with intention, discipline and a graded sequence, can become a repeatable door into the receptive state. That is the same architecture the ancient rites used, and the same architecture our apps rebuild.

Out-of-body research

The science behind OBE Induction

The modern study of the out-of-body experience begins with Celia Green, whose 1968 survey at the Institute of Psychophysical Research found that a striking share of Oxford undergraduates reported at least one. Susan Blackmore’s surveys and her 1982 monograph Beyond the Body placed the general-population figure at roughly one person in ten and argued that the experience arises when the brain’s model of where the self is located breaks down, typically at the hypnagogic edge of sleep, under deep relaxation, or in sleep paralysis.

Neurology then found the hinge. In 2002 Olaf Blanke and colleagues reported in Nature that electrical stimulation of the right angular gyrus, at the temporo-parietal junction, repeatedly produced the sensation of floating near the ceiling and seeing one’s own body below. Later work tied the experience to failures of multisensory integration: vision, touch, balance and the body schema no longer agreeing on where “I” am. In 2014 Andra Smith and Claude Messier scanned a woman who could enter an out-of-body state at will; her experience engaged left-hemisphere areas involved in kinaesthetic imagery and the body schema.

The sound laboratories approached it from the other side, as a skill. Their graded states, mind awake, body asleep; expanded awareness; the state outside time, were designed to hold the practitioner at exactly the threshold this research describes: the body offline, awareness intact. OBE Induction builds its core protocol on that map, and its EEG edition, Ritual, records the band power and gamma activity around each reported separation and return so that practitioners can study their own transitions.

IV · Before electronics

The drum and the temple

Entrainment is not a modern invention. It is the oldest ritual technology there is.

The drum · about four beats a second

Shamanic rhythm

Shamanic traditions across the world use a steady, rapid drumbeat to carry the journeyer out of ordinary consciousness, and the tempo they favour falls in the theta range. In 2026 a team at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem published the clearest test yet: forty participants listened to ten-minute drumming sessions at delta, theta and alpha tempos while their EEG was recorded. The brain tracked the theta-rate drum most closely, and the strength of that theta tracking predicted how deeply each person’s consciousness was altered.

The authors concluded that theta activity may not only accompany altered states but actively help produce them.

Shaman
The chamber · about 110 Hz

Temple acoustics

Acoustic surveys of ancient chambers, from Neolithic passage tombs to the rock-cut Hypogeum of Malta, have found strong resonances clustered roughly between 95 and 120 Hz, in the range of an adult male voice. When researchers at UCLA played tones across that range to volunteers wearing EEG, 110 Hz stood apart: activity over the left temporal language areas fell, and prefrontal activity shifted from left-dominant to right-dominant.

Chant in a resonant stone room may have been tuning the brain long before anyone could measure it. Our temple soundscapes are built with that in mind.
